# Sound First, Defer Characters

A single principle runs through all 34 "Get Speaking Mandarin" Q&A sessions: **install the sound system before the writing system, and attach meaning early but never let it crowd out phonology.** Characters are a writing system layered onto a language you should already be able to say. Learn to speak first; the characters then anchor effortlessly onto words your mouth and ear already own.

This page is the durable distillation. Read it as rules for your own practice and as generation constraints for any AI building graded readings or vocab.

## The Core Claim

- **Writing systems cannot put sound in your head — they only excite sounds already there.** If the audio isn't mastered, romanization silently defaults to your native phonemes. Pinyin/Bopomofo is annotation, not primary material.
- **Sound is the foundation; characters are whipped cream.** Learners who treat characters as primary and pronunciation as decoration build fossilized phonetic habits that cost years to undo. Learn correctly from the start — it is orders of magnitude cheaper than retraining.
- **The adult disadvantage is self-inflicted.** Children and adults both take years; children simply prioritize sound while adults over-prioritize meaning and lose phonetic detail. Front-loading sound recovers the adult's analytic advantage without paying the fossilization tax.
- **Cautionary case:** a fluent American Japanese speaker with terrible pronunciation — he learned vocabulary first. Sound-first exists to prevent exactly this outcome.

## Sequence of Acquisition

Work the stages in order. Do not chase a later stage while an earlier one is unstable.

1. **Produce the sound correctly** — full attention on articulation and contour.
2. **Notice sound distinctions** — your ear sharpens; you start hearing what you couldn't.
3. **Perceive grammar** — patterns absorb during memorization even when not consciously understood.
4. **Grasp meaning** — semantic saturation comes once the phonological scaffold holds.

- **Defer meaning within a rep cycle, not across the lesson.** Know the gloss before you start a lesson, then drop conscious meaning-tracking during each chorus so all attention goes to cadence. Meaning attached *early at the lesson level* (English-gloss-first, then Chinese) prevents fossilization; meaning chased *inside a single rep* steals attention from pronunciation. Hold both: gloss before, contour during.
- **Tolerate ambiguity.** Operating comfortably in partial understanding is a hallmark of effective learners. Tensing up for full comprehension blocks the phonetic contours from landing.

## Chorusing Is the Engine

Chorusing — speaking *simultaneously* over native audio — is the primary acquisition tool. It builds procedural sound-pattern memory that reading or reasoning about tones cannot.

- **Why chorus over shadow:** chorusing needs only ~1 syllable of working memory, so it scales to long sentences where shadowing collapses. Use shadowing for longer comprehension-forcing material; chorus for phonetic output. Either is valid — experiment to find your style.
- **Repetition is the mechanism, not lazy advice.** Practice until you *can't get it wrong*, not until you get it right once. Reps reveal ever-finer detail: after 20–50 reps a contour you missed suddenly appears — your ear sharpened, the audio never changed. Keep going.
- **The cost of fossilization is concrete:** to fix a 20-year wrong habit you must chorus it *right* more times than you've said it *wrong* — potentially millions of reps. Learning correctly from the start avoids the debt entirely.
- **Order within sound:** lock prosodic contour first (hum the melody/intonation), then layer tones, then fine consonant/vowel distinctions. Rhythm alignment is the gateway to tone accuracy. Early on, use spaced repetition for melody/rhythm (you can analyze isolated sounds), then shift to chorusing for consonant/vowel precision.
- **For new sounds, chorus single-syllable words, not sentences.**
- **Pinyin-before-characters drill:** write your *hypothesis* of what you hear in pinyin first, then listen again to catch mispronunciations, then add characters.

## Tones Are Relative Contours

Tones are relative pitch *shapes*, not absolute pitches. They shift with adjacent tones, sentence position, breath energy, and emotion. Tone diagrams mislead by drawing fixed pitches that don't exist in natural speech.

- **Don't analyze tones while speaking.** Mimic the target voice holistically. Even native speakers must say a word to themselves to label its tone — tones are *output*, not input to thought.
- **Aim for ~70–80% accuracy first.** Context carries comprehension; perfect tones come later. Perfectionism is paralysis.
- **Tone accuracy emerges from sentence cadence and chorusing, not isolated tone-pair drills.** You hear your own error only *after* chorusing, never before.
- **Make basic tones automatic muscle memory** so emotion and cadence can layer on top; tones fluctuate naturally with genuine emotion.

Concrete contour rules:

- **Third tone:** "half" (stays low) is always safe — use it when unsure. The "full" up-bounce appears only before a pause or for emphasis. Keep the same duration and pause as if you'd risen. Third tone is the *longest*.
- **Fourth tone:** start really high, drop fast and low — "whoa" cracking into chest voice. Shortest in duration.
- **Second tone:** *pull* the pitch up to a defined ceiling, don't *push* and hope. One learner unlocked a year of stuck progress by switching this mental model alone.
- **Auditory anchors:** 1–3 pair sounds like English "aha" (high-flat then lower). Imagery: walk *down* a mountain for tone 4, sit in a *valley* for tone 3.
- **Sentence-level reduction:** sentence-final third tones get abbreviated toward fourth; Taiwanese waiters' `hǎo` (3rd) sounds like 2nd under sentence intonation. Learn the reduced form, not just the citation form.
- **Exaggerate heavily.** Your over-exaggerated version sounds normal on playback — that gap is the perceptual gap, and it's real.
- **Learn the wide range first.** Train the broad, exaggerated PRC tone range as the foundation; compressing to Taiwanese later is easy, expanding a narrow range is hard.

## Close the Perception Gap

Most learners are convinced they're pronouncing correctly and almost never spontaneously discover they're wrong. Your real-time self-perception runs through bone conduction and differs from what comes out.

- **Record and compare side-by-side with native audio.** Place your syllable next to the native's same syllable in Audacity to surface consonant, vowel, and aspiration errors you cannot hear live. One learner did months of reps only to find the consonant, vowel, *and* aspiration were all wrong.
- **Direct real-time monitoring beats record-then-playback for immediacy** — mic into headphones (~$100 interface) catches tone errors as they happen. The single biggest feedback upgrade over audio-only practice.
- **Emotional discomfort is normal and proportional to gain.** The first ~5 corrections "feel like needles in the eyes," then level off. Push through.
- **Voice-recognition software** (Google Translate voice input) is a practical first intelligibility checkpoint before native speakers.

## Pronunciation Is Motor Learning, Not Anatomy Diagrams

- **Improve through listening granularity, not tongue-position charts** — sharpen perceptual resolution like going from 8-bit to HD. A 22-year learner produced a wrong "sh" for two decades; listening never revealed it, only failed comprehension did.
- **Mouth fatigue early is normal** (untrained muscles). Practice slowly 2–3 min/day for a week, then speed up. Mandarin uses more vertical mouth opening than English.
- **Transitions between sounds matter as much as the sounds.** A wrong starting position cascades into the next sound. Practice overlapping syllable pairs (1–2, 2–3, 3–4) before the full sentence.
- **Pick ONE regional accent and stay consistent while learning.** Mixing regions sounds bizarre and signals disfluency. Switch only after mastering one. Regional variation (r, w/v shifts, LE usage) is authentic input to decode, not error to correct.
- **Languages have different default mouth postures** — switching languages requires resetting the base tension.

## Characters Come Later — and Then Easily

- **Defer character study until after the speaking course.** Learning characters too early builds bad phonetic habits. Once the sound system is solid, vocabulary expansion is fast and characters anchor onto words you already say.
- **Most characters are sound-component-based, not meaning-first.** Teaching them meaning-first slows learning. Once you know ~3,000–4,000 characters, component/radical inference unlocks context-guessing the way it does in European languages.
- **When you do read, read characters — not romanization — while listening**, to disambiguate homophones and reinforce grammatical form. Latin script lets the brain default to alphabetic processing and bypass tone internalization.

## Rules for AI Generating Readings and Vocab

These follow directly from sound-first and are non-negotiable generation constraints:

- **Audio-first, always.** Never present a Chinese word without its audio. Vocab cards: audio cue → character answer. Reading-only vocab creates pronunciation gaps with no audio reference to fall back on.
- **No romanization on cards.** Use pure character or zhuyin. Pinyin was designed for native speakers learning to write; on cards it triggers alphabetic processing and bypasses tone internalization. Pinyin is acceptable only as a transient *annotation* layer, never as primary content.
- **Use natural-speed native audio with accurate transcripts.** Do not artificially slow speech — slowing distorts prosody, tone contours, and word boundaries. If pacing is needed, slow playback at runtime (0.8–0.9×), not the source.
- **Store sentences and context, not isolated words.** The sentence is the retrieval pathway; isolated entries fail to trigger recall. Embed vocab in narrative.
- **Mine vocabulary only from already-consumed material.** Pull from completed lessons and finished readers, never unknown sources — incidental new vocab derails target practice. Gate HSK words until they've actually appeared.
- **Gloss before, cadence during.** Provide the meaning up front so the learner enters a lesson knowing it, then let them drop meaning-tracking while chorusing. Generation should make the gloss available *before* the audio, not interleaved into it.
- **Sequence sound before sense in card design,** but attach English meaning at the lesson level early to prevent fossilization. Both, in the right scope.
- **Target ~80% comprehensibility.** Master ~80% of a dialogue's vocab before it's set for chorusing, or chorusing degrades into empty pronunciation with no semantic anchors. If >25% of characters are new, the text is too hard.
- **Offer multiple speaker voices on the same sentences** (4+) to train regional-variation tolerance and hold attention better than one voice repeated.
- **Provide raw audio and let the learner process it** — chorus window plus repeat window, loopable, silence-segmentable. Respect learner autonomy over rep counts.
