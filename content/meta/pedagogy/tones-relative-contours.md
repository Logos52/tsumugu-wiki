Tones in Mandarin are relative pitch *shapes*, not absolute pitches. They bend with neighboring tones, position in the sentence, breath energy, and emotion. The five-line tone diagram is a teaching fiction: the fixed pitches it draws do not occur in natural speech. This page distills the recurring guidance across 34 "Get Speaking Mandarin" Q&A sessions, where tone questions surface in nearly every session.

## The Core Claim

- A tone is a **contour relative to the surrounding contours**, not a note you hit. The same tone on the same word lands at different absolute pitches depending on what came before, where it sits in the sentence, and how loud/emotional the breath is.
- Tone diagrams mislead. They show stable pitches that "don't exist in natural speech." Treat them as a rough mnemonic, never as a target to reproduce literally.
- Tones are **output, not input to thought**. Don't compute a tone while speaking — mimic the target voice holistically. Even native speakers have to say the word to themselves before they can label its tone.
- Tone accuracy emerges from **sentence-cadence and chorusing practice**, not from isolated tone-pair drills or conscious mouth-mechanics. You typically hear your own tone error only *after* chorusing the native line, never before.

## Per-Tone Working Rules

- **Tone 3 (dipping):** The "full" up-bounce form occurs only before a pause or for emphasis. The "half" form (stays low, no rise) is *always safe* — use it whenever unsure. Keep the same duration and pause as if you had risen. Third tone is the **longest** tone.
- **Tone 4 (falling):** Start really high and drop fast and low — a "whoa" cracking down into chest voice. It is the **shortest** in duration.
- **Tone 2 (rising):** **Pull** the pitch up to a defined ceiling rather than **push** and hope you land somewhere. One learner unlocked a year of stalled progress purely by switching the mental model from push to pull.
- **Tone 1 (high level):** A sustained ceiling note; it sets the reference height the other tones move relative to.

## Position and Context Override the Citation Form

- **Sentence-final third tones get abbreviated** until they sound almost like fourth tones. Taiwanese waiters' 好 (3rd) often sounds like 2nd (háo) under sentence intonation. Learn the *contextual* realization, not just the dictionary tone.
- A tone's absolute pitch is reset by its neighbor. Tones must be practiced **in pairs and in context** (~5 min/day), because that is the unit real speech operates in — isolated single-tone drills teach a form that never occurs.
- Concrete auditory anchors:
  - The **1–3 pair** sounds like English "aha" — high-flat, then dropping lower.
  - Mental imagery: **walk down a mountain for tone 4** (sustained controlled descent), **sit in a valley for tone 3** (stay low).

## How to Actually Acquire Tones

- **Make the basic tones automatic muscle memory first**, so emotion and cadence can layer on top. Tones fluctuate naturally with genuine emotion — that's a feature, and it only works once the baseline is automatic.
- **Aim for ~70–80% tone accuracy before chasing perfection.** Context carries comprehension; perfect tones come later. Perfectionism here causes paralysis and kills the consistency that actually fixes tones.
- **Exaggerate heavily.** Because of the perceptual gap, an over-exaggerated rendition sounds *normal* on playback. Push the range further than feels natural, then check the recording.
- Lock **rhythm and melody first** (hum the intonation), then layer tones onto the established prosodic frame. Rhythm alignment is the gateway to tone accuracy, not a separate later step.
- **Learn the wide tonal range first.** The wider, exaggerated mainland (PRC) tone range is the better foundation: it is easier to *compress* to a flatter Taiwanese range later than to *expand* a narrow one. Start wide regardless of eventual target accent.

## What Not to Do

- Don't analyze tones consciously mid-sentence. Conscious mouth-mechanics during speech degrade fluency and don't improve accuracy.
- Don't drill isolated tone pairs pre-emptively, especially early. The real problem is almost always **sentence-level cadence at speed** — do dialogue/chorusing review first, then target cadence work on those same sentences.
- Don't trust your live self-perception. Bone conduction makes your tones sound more correct to you than they are. Record and compare side-by-side with the native line (e.g. in Audacity) to surface the error.
- Don't slow native audio to "hear the tones better." Slowing distorts the very contours, boundaries, and reductions you're trying to learn. Use natural-speed audio.

## Takeaways for Graded-Reading and Vocab Generation

- **Never present tones as fixed pitches** in generated material. Where a tone diagram or pitch description is included, frame it as a relative contour and flag that it shifts by position and neighbor.
- **Store and teach tones in context (sentence/pair), never in isolation.** Vocab entries and example sentences should carry the contextual realization, not just the citation tone — especially for sentence-final 3rd tones and 3rd-tone sandhi.
- **Pair every tone target with native audio.** Tone is acquired by chorusing a contour, not by reading a tone number. Audio-first cards (audio → character) beat tone-annotation-first cards.
- **Don't strip tones to a number and call it taught.** A "tone 3" label is a post-hoc abstraction; the durable representation is the heard contour in its sentence.
- **Default vocab and reader audio to the wide (PRC) tonal range** as the learning foundation, with the option to compress toward Taiwanese prosody later.
- **Calibrate expectations toward ~70–80% accuracy** in any tone-feedback or scoring, not perfection. Reserve perfectionist scoring for the polish stage, not acquisition.
