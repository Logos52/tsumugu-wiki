Distilled from the recurring themes across 34 "Get Speaking Mandarin" Q&A sessions, where questions about ordering, pacing, and "when do I add X?" came up in nearly every session. The throughline: **what you study matters less than the order you study it in.** A weak lower level silently caps every level above it, and most stalls trace back to skipping or rushing an earlier stage.

## The Level Ladder

GSM is built as a deliberate scaffold. Each level assumes the one below is solid.

- **GSM1 — sound first.** Pronunciation-focused short listening chunks. The job is phonology: syllables, cadence, pitch. Meaning is secondary.
- **GSM2 — full conversations.** HSK2–3 vocabulary, conversation structure, chorusing of complete dialogues. This is where speaking competence is actually built.
- **GSM3 — grammar structures.** Patterns aligned to MTC (台師大 Mandarin Training Center) textbooks. Grammar gets named only after the sound and conversation layers hold.
- **Graded readers** sit alongside, extending vocabulary and pushing toward discourse-length input.

**Rule:** Finish GSM2 (through lesson 10) before starting GSM3. Most weak GSM3 retention traces directly to under-practiced GSM2 — the conversation layer was never automatic, so the grammar layer has nothing to anchor to.

## Sequence Within the Method

The order of *activities* matters as much as the order of *levels*.

- **Shadowing/chorusing before pronunciation correction.** Do the self-discovery work first. Hearing your own gap is what motivates the correction effort; correcting before the learner feels the problem is wasted instruction.
- **Dialogue review before isolated tone/cadence work.** The real problem is almost always sentence-level cadence at speed, not isolated tones. Fix the sentence, and the tones come along.
- **Don't pre-emptively drill tone pairs.** Finish GSM2–3 dialogue review first, then do tone/cadence work *on those lessons' own sentences.* Isolated tone-pair drilling ahead of context solves a problem the learner doesn't yet have.
- **Master ~80% of a dialogue's vocabulary before chorusing it.** Chorus without semantic anchors and it degrades into empty pronunciation — sound with no meaning to hold it.

## Hold Grammar Anxiety During GSM1

The single most common sequencing mistake: reaching for grammar while motor control is still unstable.

- During the GSM1 pronunciation phase, **defer all LE / particle / substitution questions to GSM2.** Memorizing rules while tone production is wobbly causes overload and *slows* tone accuracy — you can only optimize one aspect per session.
- Grammar absorbs implicitly during memorization anyway. Patterns land as "this sounds right" long before you can state the rule. Naming the rule too early adds cognitive load without adding ability.
- Tolerate ambiguity. Operating in partial understanding is a hallmark of effective learners, not a gap to plug immediately.

## Broad First, Then Deep

Two passes beat one perfect pass.

- **First pass: go broad at ~30–40% depth** across all lessons. This builds cross-context recognition — you start hearing the same patterns and words recur, which makes each one easier to lock later.
- **Second pass: circle back for chorusing and production** on the items that aren't yet fluent.
- When revisiting a level, **target only the unfamiliar or non-fluent items.** Skip what's already mastered; spaced repetition handles underlying retention automatically. Re-chorusing mastered material is motion without progress.
- **Redo early lessons later.** You will hear things you couldn't hear before — finer tone contours, consonant detail. Don't assume you're "done" with a lesson because you passed through it once.

## Pacing and Load Within a Level

Difficulty escalates on purpose, and struggle is the progression signal.

- Problem-sentence count climbs across a level (roughly 1 hard sentence at lesson 11 → 4 by lesson 16). This is the level working, not the learner failing.
- **Isolate problem sentences and repeat them 50+ times.** Place easy sentences between hard ones as in-session mental breaks. Put tedious material (long number sequences) at the *end* to preserve momentum.
- Mental load decreases over roughly 11–12 lessons as control automates — the "learning to drive" curve. Early-lesson fatigue is the sore-muscle signal to keep going, not to stop.
- **Split long dialogues for pacing.** Chunk a longer GSM2 dialogue into two shorter GSM3-style dialogues. Longer pieces suit shadowing (comprehension-forcing); shorter, more numerous pieces suit chorusing (phonetic output).
- Consistency beats intensity: 15 min/day beats five-hour sessions with two-week gaps. Stop at cognitive fatigue (~30 min); split into 2–4 short bursts. Sleep consolidates motor learning, so distributing across days beats marathons.

## Don't Bolt On Supplementary Material

The scaffold is self-sufficient while foundations are forming.

- **GSM1–3 + graded readers suffice** for the core build. External vocab books and internet mining inject untranslated vocabulary that needs weeks of side-learning and derails target practice.
- **Mine vocabulary only from material you've already consumed** — completed lessons and finished readers. Pulling from unknown sources floods you with incidental new words. Add HSK words only once they've actually appeared in a lesson.
- Graded readers add ~60 new words per level by design. Match the text to proficiency: target ~80% comprehensibility, and if >25% of characters are new, the text is too hard — build frequency flashcards first.

## Planning Across the Arc (Taiwan-bound)

- **Finish GSM2 before arriving in Taiwan.** You want conversation structure automatic before you're under live communicative pressure.
- **Enroll one school level below your study level.** This leaves room for review without overwhelm — the cost of overshooting placement is steep, the cost of slight review is near zero.
- **Replay earlier-level graded readers** to verify progress and diagnose remaining gaps: a reader you can now read easily tells you whether what's left to fix is vocabulary or grammar. Each needs different remediation.

## The Discourse Ceiling

GSM's sentence-level design has a known limit, and the sequence should anticipate it.

- ~1,000 short sentences build a strong foundation but hit a ceiling. Real transfer to graded readers and authentic audio needs **longer, syntactically complex material.**
- After foundational sentence mastery, the progression should grow toward **discourse-level input** — paragraphs and connected speech, not just more isolated sentences.
- This is also where production becomes the bridge: reverse the drill (English cue → produce Chinese) to surface word-order, aspect-marker, and time-clause gaps that passive chorusing leaves hidden.

## Guidance for AI Generating Readings and Vocab

When generating graded content or sequencing a learner's path, encode the scaffold:

- **Respect the level gate.** Don't introduce GSM3-style grammar framing or named rules into GSM1/early-GSM2 material — keep early material sound-first with meaning known in advance but not analyzed mid-rep.
- **Cap new vocabulary against what's been consumed.** Default to ~50–70% review + 30–50% new, anchored to HSK3 with slight deliberate overshoot. Never source new words from outside the learner's completed corpus.
- **Sequence breadth before depth.** Generate light cross-context exposure first; reserve dense chorusing/production targets for a return pass on non-fluent items.
- **Build toward discourse.** Once short-sentence mastery is established, generate longer, more complex passages rather than more of the same length — that's the bottleneck for transfer to authentic material.
- **One new aspect per unit.** Introduce one grammar point, one meaning per word, one difficulty axis at a time; revisit the full system later. Stacking aspects causes the same overload that derails human learners.
