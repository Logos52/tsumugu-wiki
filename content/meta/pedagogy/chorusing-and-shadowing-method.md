Distilled from the recurring themes across 34 "Get Speaking Mandarin" Q&A sessions. The same principles surface again and again; this page captures the durable consensus, not any single answer.

## The Core Claim

- **Chorusing — speaking *simultaneously* over native audio — is the primary acquisition tool.** It builds procedural, motor-level sound-pattern memory that reading about tones or analyzing diagrams cannot.
- **Repetition is the actual mechanism, not lazy advice.** Pronunciation, vocab recall, and fluency all yield to enough reps. Practice until you *can't get it wrong*, not just until you get it right.
- Repetition reveals ever-finer phonetic detail: after 20–50 reps you suddenly hear a contour you missed. Your ear sharpened; the audio never changed. Keep going.
- To fix a 20-year-old wrong habit, you must chorus it *right* more times than you ever said it wrong — likely millions of reps. Learning correctly from the start is vastly cheaper. This is the strongest argument for sound-first sequencing.
- Order of attention while drilling: **produce sound → notice distinctions → perceive grammar → grasp meaning.** Don't chase meaning during early reps; pronunciation needs all the attention.

## Chorus vs. Shadow

- **Chorus** = speak *over* the audio at the same time. **Shadow** = speak just *behind* it, echoing. Both are valid; effectiveness depends on style and sentence length.
- **Chorus scales to long sentences**: it needs only ~1 syllable of working memory because you're tracking, not recalling. Shadowing fails on long sentences because you must hold the whole chunk in memory first.
- For **shadowing**, use longer pieces — it forces comprehension. For **chorusing**, use shorter, more numerous pieces — it forces phonetic output.
- Some learners find chorusing drowns out their own voice and prefer shadowing. **Experiment to find your style.** Both routes are anchored to native mimicry.
- For genuinely new sounds, chorus **single-syllable words**, not sentences.

## The Stack: Rhythm → Tone → Segments

Lock the layers in order. Each is the gateway to the next.

- **Rhythm/melody first.** Hum the prosodic contour, match the intonation. Rhythm alignment is the prerequisite for tone accuracy — get the melody and the tones largely follow.
- **Tones second.** Make basic tones automatic muscle memory so emotion and cadence can layer on top. Tones fluctuate naturally with genuine emotion; that only works once they're automatic.
- **Fine consonant/vowel distinctions last.** These are precision work, best done once melody and tone are stable.
- Use repetition/SRS *early* for melody and rhythm (you can analyze individual sounds when they're spaced out); shift to chorusing *later* for consonant/vowel precision and fossilization fixes.

## Signal That You're Doing It Right

- **Vowel pitch-lock with the speaker.** When your vowel resonates *with* theirs — felt in mouth, throat, and chest, like singing on-key — you're chorusing correctly. Closed-ear headphones capture this bone resonance and make the lock audible.
- **Direct audio monitoring** (mic into headphones during chorusing, ~$100 interface) gives real-time feedback that closes the gap between intended and actual output. This beats record-then-playback for immediacy and is the single biggest upgrade over audio-only practice.
- **Exaggerate heavily.** Your over-exaggerated version sounds *normal* on playback — that perceptual gap is the point.

## Volume and Format

- Concrete target from the course design: ~1000 sentences total (≈50 sentences × ~24 lessons × ~2 reps), and a working format of **10 choruses + 10 repeats per sentence**. The instructor wanted 50 reps with zero gaps; 10+10 was the compromise.
- Calibrating points from real learners: ~1000 reps per pitch pattern was enough to spot native errors "like a sore thumb"; learners have run ~1000 reps per lesson while distance running, or looped an hour-long podcast on infinite repeat.
- Heavy repetition (500+ choruses) produces **unconscious vocab recall you never explicitly studied** — the sound carries the word in.
- Make it sustainable: switching from reading-tones-aloud (exhausting) to listen-and-repeat turned a draining practice into a fun, sustainable 30-minute session.

## Prerequisite: Know the Material Before You Chorus

- **Master ~80% of a dialogue's vocabulary *before* chorusing it.** Without semantic anchors, chorusing degrades into empty pronunciation drill.
- Resolution of the "defer meaning" tension: defer meaning *within a single rep cycle* so you can focus on cadence — but **know the gloss before you start the lesson.**
- Chorus only material you've already consumed (course lessons, completed graded readers). Pulling sentences from unknown sources floods you with incidental vocab and derails the drill.

## Guidance for the Reading/Vocab Generator

When generating graded readings and vocab cards that will be chorused, encode these constraints:

- **Provide raw, natural-speed native audio with accurate character transcripts.** Never artificially slow textbook speech — slowing distorts prosody, tone contours, and word boundaries. (A learner may slow to 0.8–0.9× on day one and ramp up; the *source* stays natural.)
- **Pair every dialogue with chorus-ready audio** segmented per sentence, so reps-per-sentence are customizable (Audacity silence-detection at >0.4s gaps auto-segments). Offer a chorus window + a repeat window.
- **Audio-first vocab cards** (audio cue → character answer). Never present a Chinese word the learner hasn't heard first.
- **No romanization on cards.** Latin script lets the brain default to alphabetic processing and bypass tone internalization. Use pure characters or zhuyin; pinyin is annotation for recall, not primary material.
- **Store sentences/context, not isolated words** — the sentence is the retrieval pathway. Narrative embedding beats word lists.
- **Gate vocab to already-consumed material**: only surface words that have appeared in lessons or completed readers.
- **Sequence chunks for chorusing**: sliding-window syllable pairs (1–2, 2–3, 3–4) and their *transitions* before the full sentence. Transitions between sounds matter as much as the sounds; a wrong starting position cascades.
- **Build for ~80% comprehensibility.** If >25% of characters are new, the text is too hard to chorus productively.
- **Multiple speakers (4+ voices) on the same sentences** train the ear for regional variation and hold attention better than one voice repeated. Pick one regional accent as the spine and keep it consistent within a learning track.

## Durable Takeaways

- Chorusing is the engine; everything else is scaffolding for it.
- Repeat past "right" to "can't get it wrong." Finer detail keeps emerging — that's your ear improving, so don't stop early.
- Lock rhythm, then tone, then segments. Don't skip the melody.
- Real-time self-monitoring (mic + headphones) is the feedback loop that makes reps count.
- Know the meaning before you start; defer it during the rep.
- Generated material must be natural-speed, audio-first, character-only, drawn from what the learner already knows, and segmented for customizable reps.
