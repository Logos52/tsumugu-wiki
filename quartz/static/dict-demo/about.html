How to read these pages

Every character page is built around one idea: you remember a character by watching its pieces fit together. The sections below are the keys to the codes those pages use.

字源 and 故事 — two cards, two jobs

Each entry leads with a pair of cards.

字源 — what the parts really do. This card tells you what each component actually contributes to the character: which part carries the meaning, which part carries the sound, which part is only a leftover shape. It states only what is known.

故事 — a scene to remember it by. This card gives you a scene built from the same components, arranged the way the character is arranged, so you can redraw the glyph from the story. A story is allowed to use what a component looks like today rather than what it originally was — that is often what makes it stick. When the history is different from the look, the 字源 card is where the history lives.

Read them as a pair. One is true; the other is memorable; you always know which is which. Sometimes the truth itself is the best hook — 造's everyday meanings ride the sound of 告, and reading the walking foot 辶 as "the road to creation" is a story, not an analysis. Both cards stay useful, because each one is labeled.

The component colors

Components are typed by the job they do in this character:

  • 形 form — depicts a thing. The component is a picture doing pictorial work (the hand 寸 in 射).
  • 義 meaning — lends its sense (the quarreling 鬥 in 鬧).
  • 聲 sound — lends its sound (the 告 in 造). Sound components link to their series page.
  • 空 empty — does no work here. A mark, or a corruption: a shape that drifted until it looks like something it never was.
  • No color, no chip — the page makes no claim about that part. Read it by its modern look; the colored parts carry the analysis.

Why decomposition stops at functional components

Modern shapes can lie. The left side of 射 looks exactly like 身 "body" — but the early forms drew a bow with an arrow on the string, and centuries of copying wore the bow into a body-shaped fossil. If you decompose by what the modern shape resembles, you memorize a falsehood; if you decompose by what each component does, the character opens up. That is why every page types components by function, and why a corrupted shape gets the 空 mark instead of a made-up job.

The meaning tree

One character usually carries a family of senses, and the family has a shape:

  • (orig.) — the original sense, where the family starts.
  • COMMON — the sense you'll meet most often; anchor this one first.
  • → and ⇒ — each arrow is one more step of extension from the sense above it; ⇒ marks the second step out.
  • 〇 — borrowed for its sound: the senses on either side of this node are not logically connected. Don't look for the logic; learn the sound bridge.

Sound series and drift bands

Many components donate their sound to a whole family of characters. A series page takes one donor (告) and lines up its family in drift bands: first the members that still sound exactly like the donor (誥, 郜), then the ones whose initial made one regular shift (浩, 皓 — g → h), then the ones that drifted further while the rhyme held (造 — g → z, rhyme -ào intact). The ordering is the lesson: initials drift in bands, rhymes survive. Use the cards as guesses — read the glyph, commit to a sound, then tap to check. After two or three series, you start predicting characters you've never studied.

Stroke order

Stroke order is compositional: write the components, know the assembly rules, and the full character follows. The rules live here, once:

  1. Top before bottom.
  2. Left before right.
  3. Horizontal before crossing vertical.
  4. Enclosures come first. A full frame opens, its contents go in, and the bottom stroke seals it last (回、國). An open-bottom frame like 鬥 or 門 is written completely before what it holds: 鬧 is all of 鬥, then 市.
  5. The wrappers 辶 and 廴 are written last — in 造, 告 comes first, then the sweep of 辶 carries it away.

Each component's own stroke order lives on that component's page. A few characters violate compositional assembly (必 is the classic case); those entries carry their own one-line writing sequence.
